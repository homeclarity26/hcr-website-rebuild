Bathroom Renovation Cost in Northeast Ohio: Honest 2026 Numbers by Scope

April 2026 · By Adam Kilgore

Bathroom renovations are the project I get asked about most — and the one where I see the widest range of outcomes. A bathroom done well adds real value to your home and to your daily life. A bathroom done with a rushed scope, cut-rate tile work, or a contractor who didn't disclose what was behind the walls — that's a problem that follows you for years.

Here are real cost ranges for Northeast Ohio bathrooms in 2026. These are numbers I've seen on actual Summit County, Portage County, and Medina County projects — not national cost-calculator averages that have no relationship to your local market.

What Moves the Number Most

Tile selection and square footage

Tile is the most visible cost variable in bathroom work. Ceramic tile runs $2–$5 per square foot. Porcelain is $3–$12. Natural stone (marble, travertine, quartzite) is $8–$25+. Installation labor in Northeast Ohio runs $8–$18 per square foot depending on format size, pattern, and substrate prep required. A 72 sq ft custom tile shower can run $6,000–$12,000 in tile and labor alone — before the glass enclosure.

Shower vs. tub-shower combo

A custom walk-in shower costs more to build than a tub-shower combo — but it's also what most buyers want in a primary bath. If this is your master bathroom and you plan to sell in the next decade, a walk-in shower is the right call for most Summit County homes priced above $350,000. For guest baths and homes with young children, a tub-shower combo makes more sense.

What's behind the walls

This is the variable no bid captures — and every experienced contractor in Northeast Ohio knows it's real. In homes built before 1985, I routinely find: galvanized supply pipes at 60–80% occlusion, floor joists with moisture damage under the existing tub, inadequate exhaust ventilation that has compromised the subfloor, and knob-and-tube wiring on bathroom circuits. None of these show up in a bid. They show up when the walls come down.

A real number: One in three bathroom renovations I assess in Northeast Ohio homes built before 1975 has a plumbing or structural issue that adds $3,500–$9,000 to the final cost. Not because the contractor is padding — because the problem is real and was genuinely hidden until demo. A good scope of work builds in a contingency and a clear process for handling what's found during demolition.

Permits and inspections

Bathroom renovations involving plumbing or electrical changes require permits in Summit County and throughout Northeast Ohio. This is not optional. A contractor who suggests skipping permits to save you money is not protecting your interests — they're protecting themselves from accountability. Permit fees run $200–$800. The inspections are how you confirm the work was done correctly. Always permit.

The Sequencing Problem

The most expensive mistake in bathroom renovation: doing the cosmetic work first. You retile the floor, install new fixtures, put in a new vanity — and then discover the exhaust fan has been venting into the attic (not outside) for 22 years. Now you're pulling up new tile to fix a ventilation problem.

The correct sequence, every time: rough work first (plumbing, electrical, ventilation), then substrate and waterproofing, then tile and backer, then fixtures and finishes. Any contractor who proposes a different order is telling you something about their process.

Waterproofing Is Not Optional

The second most common issue I find in bathroom assessments of Summit County homes: inadequate waterproofing behind tile in shower surrounds. A shower with failed waterproofing looks fine from the outside — until the moisture has been migrating into the wall cavity for three to five years. By the time you see a problem, the damage is substantial.

In 2026, there is no reason to build a shower without a proper waterproofing membrane (Schluter Kerdi, Laticrete Hydro Ban, or equivalent). If a contractor says "we do it the way we've always done it," ask specifically what they use for waterproofing. The answer will tell you a lot.
