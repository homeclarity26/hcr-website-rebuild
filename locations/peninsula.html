Why Peninsula Is Different

19th-century homes have their own logic. Most advisors don't know it. Adam does.

Peninsula is a historic village in the Cuyahoga Valley National Park corridor, with a concentration of 19th-century homes unlike anywhere else in Summit County. These structures have their own construction logic — material layering built up over generations, original systems that have been partially updated but never fully replaced, settlement patterns that look alarming to someone who doesn't understand them.

A standard contractor gives you one of two reactions to a historic home: either everything is fine (because they want the job) or everything needs to be replaced (because that's a bigger job). Neither is calibrated to what an 1870 or 1900 structure actually needs.

Adam has personal experience restoring a historic farmhouse — the kind of experience that teaches you what conditions are normal for the era, which interventions are urgent, and how to write renovation scopes that respect the character of the structure while bringing it to a safe, functional, and comfortable standard.

Common Questions

Peninsula homeowners ask us:

We got a home inspection that flagged dozens of things. How do we know what's actually urgent?

This is exactly the situation the Report addresses for Peninsula homeowners. A code inspector flags every non-conforming condition regardless of whether it matters in context. Adam's assessment identifies what requires immediate remediation, what is cosmetic, and what is simply characteristic of the era. The difference between those categories determines your entire project plan and budget.

We want to modernize the home without losing what makes it special. Can the Report help with that tension?

Yes — and this is one of the most common conversations Adam has with Peninsula homeowners. He writes renovation scopes that address function and safety while being specific about which character elements to preserve and which modern substitutions are appropriate. He understands that "updating" a historic home and "ruining" a historic home are often one bad decision apart.

Should we be alarmed by what a home inspector flagged, or is that normal for a historic home?

Almost always, a code inspection on a 19th-century structure produces a document that is alarming and largely useless. The inspector applies current code standards to a structure built under entirely different logic. Adam approaches historic properties as an expert in what's typical for the era — understanding which conditions are acceptable and which are genuinely urgent — instead of generating a checklist that treats original materials as violations.
